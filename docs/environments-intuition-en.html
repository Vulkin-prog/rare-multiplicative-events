01 / 05

A tiny phase error can matter

Imagine a hand pointing somewhere on a clock face. At ordinary primes, its direction is selected uniformly at random, like spinning the hand. For a few chosen primes we set the direction ourselves. Multiplication adds angles: the direction at 2d is the direction at d, rotated by the angle prescribed at 2.

We count directions landing in a very small highlighted arc. As we inspect more integers, the arc becomes narrower. A rotation that looks negligible on the whole clock face can therefore be as large as the entire target. This paper asks what changes when our prescribed rotations vary while the target shrinks.

02 / 05

One random source can create several hits

Take the prime 2 and write an integer as an odd part times a power of two. The odd part supplies a random direction. Multiplying it by 2, then 4, then 8 creates a chain of descendants, each with one more prescribed rotation. After zooming in on the target, these rotations become steps of size c. One source can trigger several hits if their shifted target intervals overlap.

The horizontal positions stay different: hits at d,2d,4d do not collapse into one point. “Cluster” means a shared cause, not necessarily nearby locations. Integers made only from the prescribed primes have no random source left; remove those exact hits first.

03 / 05

Turn the overlap down

If the zoomed rotation is zero, descendants use the same target. Increasing the rotation reduces their overlap. In the one-prime example, a step at least as wide as the target leaves only single hits per source and the limiting count is Poisson. In between, singles and doubles coexist. The plotted curve is a formula for the limiting distribution, not a simulation of a finite arithmetic sample.

04 / 05

A total can hide the geometry

Two models can have exactly the same distribution for the total number of hits but put related hits at different spatial ratios. Three times as far and one-and-a-half times as far are visibly different maps, although a counter that records only “how many” cannot tell them apart. This is why the research result retains the complete covariance across positions.

05 / 05

Move the observation window

Imagine cropping a photograph. Two related objects may both appear in the full image while only one fits inside the crop. A cluster is visible in an interior window only if its descendants fit both the angular target and the spatial window. A region can therefore show ordinary Poisson counts even when the complete interval contains clusters. Continue to the research statements for the exact two-constraint test.
