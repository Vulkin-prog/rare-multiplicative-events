Rare events in multiplicative sequences

Numbers can share a random cause without producing the same rare events. A relation may disappear when we count events, yet remain visible when we record their positions or their history.

One distinction connects the corpus

01

Source

The prime values and the multiplicative identities they impose.

02

Configuration

The rare occurrences, with their positions and marks: sign, excess length or angle.

03

Observation

What we read from that configuration: a total, a spatial map, a maximum or a history.

Applying the same observation to two similar random configurations keeps their laws similar. But a total can forget information that the map of positions retains. The small model below makes this visible.

Two Poisson margins. One shared cause.

The calculation and what it establishes

Set a=1 and b=(1−|c|)₊. In the limit, U, V and C are independent, with U,V∼Pois(1−b), C∼Pois(b). The margins are U+C and V+C. Their total is U+V+2C.

\mathbb E(A+B)=2,\quad \operatorname{Var}(A+B)=2+2b,\quad \mathbb P(A=B=0)=e^{-2+b}.

The finite law is the k-fold convolution of probabilities 1−(2−b)/k, 2(1−b)/k, b/k at 0,1,2. Its same-grid cluster comparison has total variation at most (2−b)²/k; a continuous-position target is a separate weak limit.
