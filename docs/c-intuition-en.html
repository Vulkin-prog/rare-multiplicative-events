One coin.
Numbers that shape each other.

The starting point

Toss a coin. Then multiply.

How can one toss decide the results for several numbers?

For the number 2, we toss a fair coin: heads, write +1; tails, write −1. We do the same for 3, then 5, then 7… The previous result does not change the chances of the next toss: the tosses are independent. Each time, both outcomes have a one-in-two chance.

Why these numbers? They are the : the building blocks of multiplication. A prime number is greater than 1 and cannot be written as the product of two whole numbers greater than 1. For example, 6 splits into 2 × 3, and 12 into 2 × 2 × 3. We toss the coin for the building blocks; for the other numbers, we multiply the results we already have.

An example with actual results

The coin gives −1 for 2 and +1 for 3.
Then, for 6: (−1) × (+1) = −1.
For 12: (−1) × (−1) × (+1) = +1.

We call f(n) the result assigned to the number n. Read this as “f of n”. Here, f(6) means −1. Once we know the results for 2 and 3, the result for 6 is fixed: there is randomness, but the values are linked.

What to noticeTwo factors of −1 cancel each other: their product is +1. That is why 4 = 2 × 2, 9 = 3 × 3 and every other square always keep +1. We also assign +1 to the number 1.

Statement & methodThe exact model+

f(n) = ∏p f(p)vp(n), where the f(p) are independent and equally likely to be −1 or +1. The exponent vp(n) counts the factors of p in n. Only its parity matters. This is the completely multiplicative model, sometimes called “extended Rademacher”; it does not vanish on integers that are not squarefree. The identity f(2n)=f(2)f(n) preserves dependence at every scale.

The key idea

Chance chooses the building blocks. Multiplication links the numbers.

You now know how to build the sequence. Let us see what happens when several neighbouring results share the same sign.

From a sequence to a rare event

When the same sign keeps going.

How can we count a long run just once, even if it continues?

Imagine a game where +1 is blue and −1 is orange. You look for the longest unbroken row of cells of the same colour. In the papers, this is called a constant run.

If eight blue cells follow one another, a small window of five cells can fit in four different positions. Yet there is still only one blue run. We therefore count its start, just once.

Now consider a comparison game: toss a new coin for every cell, without a multiplication rule. Requiring one more sign in the same run halves its probability each time.

A sense of the scale

With 1,024 possible starts, a run of at least 10 signs appears once on average in this comparison game. That does not guarantee one in every game.

The letter N counts the possible starts, and L is the minimum length required. The predicted average, written λ (“lambda”), is λ = N/2ᴸ. The theorem explains why the same calculation also describes long runs in our multiplicative sequence, at large scales.

The first main result

A simple law, despite the dependencies.

One run on average: how many games contain none?

If there is one long run on average, should we expect to see exactly one? No: some games contain none, others two or more.

The Poisson distribution gives the probabilities of these different counts. With an average of 1, it predicts roughly 37% of games with no runs, 37% with exactly one, and 26% with at least two. The chart below lets you change the difficulty.

The first paper shows that, for long runs at the appropriate scale, our multiplicative sequence approaches this simple model. The result even covers positions, extra lengths and signs, all at once.

How long will the longest run be?

To reach 1,024, we can double 1 ten times: 1, 2, 4, 8… up to 1,024. This number of doublings is written log₂ 1,024 = 10. The word logarithm names this reverse calculation.

The first paper places the maximum length around this number of doublings as the sequence grows. This describes its scale, not a guaranteed length in every game.

What about squares, which are always positive?

A negative run can never cross a square. But squares grow farther apart along the sequence, while the rare length grows only like the number of doublings. Most windows at that scale therefore avoid squares. The theorem establishes equal limiting rates for long positive and negative runs in a block far from the origin. This observation explains why squares do not contradict the result; the proof also controls the other arithmetic links.

A run carries three pieces of information.

Where does it start? How far does it exceed the required length? Is it positive or negative? A run of length 12, when at least 10 signs are required, has 2 extra signs. Mathematicians call this an “excess of 2”.

A run can contain 12 or 13 cells, never 12.4. Let us choose the number of doublings of N, rounded down to the nearest whole number, as the reference length.

As long as this length stays at 12, making the sequence larger adds opportunities to find a run. When it rises to 13, the requirement becomes stricter: the expected average halves. Then it starts rising again.

Behind the proof

Count what prevents independence.

Why do the links between numbers not defeat the comparison?

To understand why the simple model works, we first need to identify the hidden links between numbers.

Look at 6 = 2 × 3, 10 = 2 × 5 and 15 = 3 × 5. When we multiply them, every prime number appears twice. Their random results therefore cancel in pairs: f(6)f(10)f(15)=+1, whatever the coin tosses give.

Once the results for 6 and 10 are known, the result for 15 is fixed. This is a concrete example of dependence. Distance between two parts of the sequence alone does not guarantee their independence.

The proof counts relations of this kind between two groups of consecutive numbers. It also first reveals the results for the small primes, then studies the randomness still available on the large primes.

What the proof contributesIt does not claim that the links disappear. It shows that their combined effect on long runs is small enough to allow the Poisson comparison.
A RELATION THAT DISTANCE CANNOT REVEAL

Deliberately reverse the result for 2, 3 or 5. Two results change each time; the product of all three always remains +1.

6 × 10 × 15 = 30²
f(6) × f(10) × f(15) = +1

Each of the three pairs is independent in this example; the triple is not. Correlation between two values therefore cannot detect every constraint.

Consider 33 = 3 × 11, 34 = 2 × 17 and 35 = 5 × 7. Even after fixing the coins for 2, 3 and 5, those for 11, 17 and 7 remain available. Each acts on just one of these three numbers. We can therefore prescribe the three signs separately; every particular word has probability one in eight.

These primes are “private” within this window, but their other multiples can still change elsewhere. The full-field proof constructs this modification and then controls its effect on other windows.

Statement & methodConditioning requires a relative error bound+

If two joint distributions are at total-variation distance at most ε, and the conditioning event has target probability p>ε, Lemma 6.3 bounds the distance between the conditional distributions by ε/p. Convergence therefore requires ε/p→0, not merely ε→0. The result also applies to a future threshold path: the observation must not be too rare relative to the available precision.

The second main result

Two ways to produce the exceptional.

Why can the very beginning of the sequence be a special place?

An exceptionally long run can benefit from many opportunities… or from a special advantage right at the beginning.

Inside a very long sequence, many positions can host a run. This is like giving a rare event many chances to happen.

At position 1, there is an arithmetic shortcut. For the first ten results all to be +1, only four coins need to land heads: those for 2, 3, 5 and 7. The other results follow automatically by multiplication.

Why the beginning is special

Four heads have a one-in-16 chance. Ten separate coins all landing heads would have only a one-in-1,024 chance. The arithmetic rule therefore makes this initial run much less unlikely.

The symbol counts the primes up to L. At the very beginning, the exact probability of L positive results is 2−π(L). The paper quantifies the competition with all the opportunities further along.

Long runs V3 · Corollary 7.8a

One fixed strip, many frames.

So far, each new draw changed the sequence. We can instead toss the coins once, keep the resulting sequence, and randomly choose where to place a frame. The randomness then comes from the observation location.

For almost every sequence drawn from this model, the frequencies of the different numbers of run starts approach a Poisson law at the scales specified in Corollary 7.8a. “Almost every” does not automatically include a particular deterministic function such as Liouville.

Corollary 7.8a ↗ · What the full map retains ↗

A count and a map answer different questions

Two frames can each contain two run starts at different locations. Their totals forget that arrangement. After fixing the entire sequence, the Poisson theorem concerns this count; it does not turn the complete location map into an independent field. Remark 7.8b proves an obstruction to that second comparison.
