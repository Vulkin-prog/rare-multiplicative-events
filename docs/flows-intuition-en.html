From rare points to a story

A record emerges as you go.

At what moment do we really know a record has been broken?

Imagine a high-score board. A run of 7 cells beats the previous record of 4. Another run of 7 ties it: the first holder keeps the title. A run of 11 breaks it again.

Going from 7 to 11 creates just one new strict record episode, even though the new run is four cells longer. And as we read the sequence cell by cell, we do not yet know where it will end: we must see its eighth cell to recognize that it beats 7.

The second paper studies this story: how often the record changes, which sign holds it, and how long it stays in the lead.

Count the minutes… or the doublings?

Imagine following a match from minute 1 to minute 64. A side leading between minutes 32 and 64 dominates more than half the observed duration, even if it was not leading at the beginning.

We can choose another clock: give equal weight to 1–2, 2–4, 4–8, 8–16, 16–32 and 32–64. Each period then corresponds to a doubling. The last period accounts for only one out of six.

Applied to record holders, this distinction changes the result. Over long admissible windows, time measured in doublings is shared equally between the + and − signs. Ordinary time, by contrast, retains a share that varies from one story to another: taking the lead late can carry a great deal of weight.

Across random realizations, the limiting mean share of ordinary time is 50%. Its limiting law remains spread out, with a standard deviation of about 37.3 percentage points: 50% is an average over histories, not a share that every history approaches. The much smaller phase effect slightly modulates this distribution.

Statement & methodThe main result for records: a joint limit+

Over a window of T doublings, N counts global strict record episodes, D=N⁺−N⁻ and A integrates the sign of the first holder in logarithmic time. Under the shell-transfer assumptions of Theorem 2.3 and the initial-population assumption:

((N(Tt)−Tt/2), D(Tt), A(Tt)) / √T ⇒ BΣ(t).
Σ, in the order (N, D, A)

In particular, fluctuations of the signed imbalance and leadership time are correlated. The mean rate of 1/2 per doubling does not make integer-record counting a Poisson process.

Why about one record every two doublings? In the Poisson target, successive record heights increase by 1, 2, 3… cells with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8…: the mean increase is two cells. The maximum grows by about one cell per doubling, which explains the asymptotic record rate. This is Proposition 3.1 and Lemmas 3.2–3.3, pp. 11–12.

The counters start at zero on a late window of T doublings, with T→∞, but the previous maximum and its holder are not reset. The conditions include a retained initial population Λ2−T→∞ and shells of width h with Th→0, together with the transfer bounds of Theorem 2.3. No phase subsequence is needed for this Brownian history. It is asymptotically independent of the entire terminal field. The terminal maximum and the sign of its holder remain functions of that same field, so they are not claimed to be independent of each other.

Statement & methodWhy shells, and why M₁?+

Records and scale flows V2.1 uses Long runs V3’s complete signed field: positions, exact lengths and actual signs are retained under the stated information budget. Record limits still have their own horizon and censoring conditions.

A jump in final length can appear as several small, closely spaced steps when only a finite prefix is visible. The M₁ topology allows these steps to merge; J₁ requires a stricter matching of jumps. For physical occupation, the limiting distribution is a family indexed by the phase θ={log₂n}, along subsequences where θ converges. This phase selection concerns the terminal block, not the Brownian history. The visible maximum converges in scalar M₁ and is not tight in J₁; the sign of the first holder converges in scalar J₁.

From the diagram to the story

Now count the points in the right-hand frame over time. Each arrival raises the total; each departure lowers it. That is what the next chart shows, in another simulation of the same model.

Count arrivals and departures.

Imagine a room where visitors arrive, stay for a while, then leave. The number of people present rises and falls over time.

The model used here follows the same simple rule: arrivals occur at random, at a constant average rate, and during any short period each person has the same probability of leaving, regardless of how long they have already spent in the room. With twice as many people present, there are twice as many opportunities for a departure.

The mathematical name is an immigration–death process. Here, “death” means that a point leaves the count. The chart shows one path: an upward jump is an arrival, a downward jump is a departure.

The link with the papersThe room is an analogy for understanding a model of random points. The second paper constructs this model by extending integer lengths through a precise interpolation; it does not assume that our finite arithmetic sequence follows these arrivals and departures exactly.

Compare a small crowd with a large crowd.

With an average of 2 people, counting 0, 1 or 2 makes a big difference. With an average of 128, a difference of a few people is relatively small.

To compare these situations, first subtract the average, then divide the difference by its typical size—here, the square root of the average. The centre becomes zero and the fluctuations become comparable.

At a large average, the bars approach the familiar bell curve of the normal, or Gaussian, distribution. Values close together in time remain linked, however: seeing a crowd at one moment makes a crowd likely just afterwards.

Two scales coexist in the paper. When a threshold retains many runs, their standardized fluctuations approach a Gaussian law. At the extreme threshold, only a few runs remain, and their count keeps a Poisson law. Summarizing many thresholds can therefore give a Gaussian fluctuation even though the final threshold itself is not Gaussian (Theorems 7.5 and 7.11).

Statement & methodTwo sections of the same Poisson geometry+

In the auxiliary plane, Xc(τ)=Π((0,eτ]×(0,ce−τ]) is stationary with distribution Pois(c) and satisfies

Xc(τ+Δ) | Xc(τ)=z  ∼  Bin(z,e−Δ) + Pois(c(1−e−Δ)).

The two terms are independent. The generator is 𝒜g(z)=c[g(z+1)−g(z)]+z[g(z−1)−g(z)]. The constant-area section has an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck limit with covariance e−|τ−σ|. The normalized section following the threshold at a fixed position gives a different covariance: e−|v−w|/2. These two clocks are not interchangeable. The limit and interpolation checks are in the Poisson flows companion, Appendix C, pp. 9–12.

The canonical interpolation uses auxiliary fractional marks with density g(u)=2 ln(2)·2−u, for 0<u<1 (they are not uniform); adding these marks can create records within a tied integer level. The theorems for strict integer records are therefore treated separately.

Model, normalization and exact finite covariance

Take μ=2^(J+θ−1), a Poisson(μ) population and independent exponential lifetimes with rate log2. If N(t) is the survivor count, H(t)=(N(t)−μ2⁻ᵗ)/√(μ2⁻ᵗ). We compute C by an exact integral of those lifetimes, without quadrature.

D_J=J^{-1/2}\sum_{j=0}^J H(j),\quad C_J=J^{-1/2}\int_0^J H(t)\,dt.

With α=(log2)/2 and r=e⁻ᵅ, the covariance kernel is e^(−α|t−u|), so

v_D=\frac{J+1+2\sum_{k=1}^J(J+1-k)r^k}{J},\quad v_C=\frac2\alpha-\frac{2(1-r^J)}{J\alpha^2},
c_{DC}=\frac2{J\alpha}\left(J+1-\sum_{j=0}^Jr^j\right).

This samples the Poisson target, not the arithmetic function. Second moments are computed directly for that target. In the joint Gaussian limit, C and D−C become independent; finite J retains a correlation.
